In many Malayali homes, people show love by cooking, worrying, sending money or checking whether you've eaten. Talking about feelings, especially sadness or anxiety, is less familiar. So when you want to tell your family you're struggling, or that you're seeing a therapist, it can feel like crossing a language barrier inside your own home.

Here are some ideas that may help. Take what fits and leave the rest.

## Decide what you want from the conversation

Before you speak, ask yourself what you're hoping for. Do you want them to understand? To give you some space? To support you in seeing a therapist? Or simply to know, so you don't have to hide it?

Being clear about this helps you choose your words, and helps you notice when the conversation has done enough for now.

## Pick a calm moment

Big conversations go better when nobody is rushing, tired or already upset. A walk, a car ride or a quiet evening can feel less intense than sitting face to face.

## Use words that feel familiar

Clinical words like "depression" or "anxiety disorder" can sound frightening or foreign. You might start with something more everyday:

- "I've been feeling very tired and low for a while, and it's not getting better on its own."
- "My mind has been under a lot of tension, and I want to learn how to handle it."
- "I've started talking to someone, a counsellor, and it's been helping."

## Expect some worry, and maybe some resistance

Parents and elders might respond with fear ("What will people say?"), advice ("Just pray / exercise / think positive"), or guilt ("Did we do something wrong?"). These reactions usually come from love and unfamiliarity, not rejection.

You can acknowledge their feelings without giving up your own: "I know this is new for you. It's important to me, and I'd like your support."

## You don't need everyone's approval

If your family isn't ready to understand, you can still look after your mental health. Support can come from a friend, a cousin, a partner or a counsellor. Some people find it easier for a therapist to join a family session and help explain.

## Look after yourself afterwards

However it goes, these conversations take energy. Do something gentle for yourself afterwards. If it didn't go the way you hoped, it doesn't mean it never will. Understanding often grows slowly, over many small conversations.
